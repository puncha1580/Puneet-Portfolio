ABOUT

My work has never been solely about documentation. It has always been about helping people understand.

Across technical communication, learning design, enterprise technology, and enablement, one question has stayed with me: how do we help people make sense of something complex enough to use it with confidence?

Introduction

Over 15 years across different technology environments, I’ve returned to a recurring challenge: bridging the gap between information and understanding, and between understanding and action.

The context has changed—from banking and enterprise software to SaaS platforms and AI infrastructure. So have the formats, tools, and responsibilities. But the underlying objective has remained the same: helping people understand what they need to know and use that understanding in practice.

My approach

Clarity is not simply about explaining something well. It is about helping someone do something with what they understand.

Information can be accurate and still be difficult to use. Documentation can be comprehensive without answering the question a user actually has. Training can explain a process without preparing someone to apply it in a real situation.

I start with the people who need the information: what they are trying to accomplish, what they already know, and where they are likely to encounter friction. From there, I focus on structuring knowledge around real tasks and connecting information to the decisions and actions it needs to support.

My thinking about learning also extends beyond the workplace. Watching my son learn to interact with technology has made me reflect on the role of observation in how we acquire skills. Children often begin by watching others, experimenting for themselves, and gradually finding their own ways to interact. Much of that learning happens before anyone formally explains the process.

It has also made me think more carefully about AI. There is a difference between a tool that simply supplies an answer and one that encourages someone to explore an idea further. With children especially, I find myself asking: is AI helping them develop their own thinking, or allowing them to bypass it? The answer may depend not just on the technology, but on how we introduce it and the behaviours we encourage around it.

That question carries into my professional work. Whether I am designing documentation, a learning experience, or an AI-enabled workflow, I want to understand not just whether people can get an answer, but whether they are better equipped to act independently afterward.

The experience behind the work

My journey began in technical communication, but the questions I encountered extended beyond writing.

How does a developer find the information needed to integrate an API? How does a customer learn to use a new platform? How does an employee develop confidence with an unfamiliar process? How can an organization preserve and share knowledge as its systems and teams evolve?

Technical documentation taught me to structure information and explain systems precisely. Learning design introduced ways to turn concepts into experiences people could practise. Enablement brought the focus closer to adoption—helping people apply knowledge in the context of their work. My experience with Agile practices, documentation operations, and cross-functional teams added another dimension: understanding how knowledge, processes, and people work together.

Product thinking extends that journey by asking a related question: Are we solving the right problem in the first place?

These disciplines are connected ways of understanding what people need, where they encounter difficulty, and how better information and experiences can help them move forward.

What I bring

I bring a combination of technical communication, information architecture, learning design, and practical enablement experience.

My work spans documentation governance, structured content, developer documentation, learning experiences, and AI-assisted workflows. I have worked on documentation modernization, content migration, onboarding, guided walkthroughs, and capability development across technology-driven environments.

Information architecture gives knowledge structure. Governance helps keep it consistent and maintainable. Documentation and learning experiences make it accessible. Enablement helps people apply it. Cross-functional collaboration helps ensure the result reflects the realities of users, technical teams, and business stakeholders.

I see AI as another part of this evolving landscape—not a substitute for sound information design or human judgment, but an opportunity to rethink how knowledge is created, reviewed, delivered, and used.

What I’m exploring now

I’m increasingly interested in what happens when we look beyond the document itself and start with the questions people need answered.

This interest informs my exploration of Question-Driven Documentation (QDD): a way of thinking about documentation through the questions users ask, the problems they are trying to solve, and the answers that help them take the next step.

I’m also developing product case studies that let me examine problems through a broader product lens—from understanding the problem and framing the opportunity to thinking through the solution and its value.

Both explorations build on the same underlying interest that has shaped my work over the years: making knowledge more useful by understanding the people who need it.

The tools will continue to change. The formats will evolve. What matters to me is that people can find what they need, understand what it means, and use that understanding to move forward.
